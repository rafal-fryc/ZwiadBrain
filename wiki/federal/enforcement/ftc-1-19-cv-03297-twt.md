## Summary

The FTC charged Equifax Inc. with unfair and deceptive practices and violations of the GLBA Safeguards Rule after a 2017 data breach exposed sensitive personal information of approximately 147 million consumers. Equifax failed to patch a critical Apache Struts vulnerability for over four months, misrepresented its security practices to consumers and small businesses, and lacked basic controls including network segmentation and intrusion detection. The matter resolved through a consent order entered in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Georgia.

## Developments

- 2019-07-15 — Consent order entered against Equifax requiring $425 million in consumer relief and a 20-year information security program following the 2017 breach exposing 147 million consumers' personal data. — [source](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/172-3203-equifax-inc)

## Background

Equifax, one of the three nationwide consumer reporting agencies, suffered a massive breach in 2017 when attackers exploited a known Apache Struts vulnerability (CVE-2017-5638) that Equifax had failed to patch despite a US-CERT alert. Attackers moved laterally through dozens of unrelated databases due to absent network segmentation, plain-text administrative credentials, and no intrusion detection. Equifax's own internal documents show awareness of systemic security deficiencies since at least 2014.

## Allegations

The FTC alleged four counts: (1) unfair data security practices for failing to implement reasonable safeguards while collecting and storing sensitive consumer information; (2) deceptive representations to consumers through its direct-to-consumer privacy policy claiming reasonable physical, technical, and procedural safeguards; (3) deceptive representations to small businesses through the Equifax Small Business privacy policy making equivalent security claims; and (4) violations of the GLBA Safeguards Rule by failing to design, implement, test, and adjust an adequate information security program.

## Outcome

A consent order was entered requiring Equifax to pay a monetary judgment of $425,000,000, with at least $300,000,000 deposited into a consumer fund for restitution. Equifax must implement and maintain a comprehensive information security program for 20 years, submit to biennial third-party assessments, provide annual board-level compliance certifications, report covered incidents within ten days of notifying any government entity, offer affected consumers single-bureau credit monitoring for up to ten years, provide free security freezes for ten years, and is permanently prohibited from misrepresenting its data security practices and from violating the GLBA Safeguards Rule.

## Significance

The Equifax consent order represents one of the largest data security enforcement actions in FTC history, with $425 million in monetary relief tied to a breach affecting nearly half the U.S. adult population. The 20-year order duration, mandatory board-level certifications, and structured third-party assessment regime set a high-water mark for FTC data security remediation requirements against large financial-sector data holders.
