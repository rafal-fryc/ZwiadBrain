## Summary

The FTC charged Easy Healthcare Corporation, developer of the Premom Ovulation Tracker app, with secretly sharing hundreds of thousands of women's sensitive reproductive health and geolocation data with third-party advertising firms — including Chinese analytics companies — despite explicit privacy promises. The agency also alleged Easy Healthcare violated the Health Breach Notification Rule by failing to notify consumers, the FTC, or the media of security breaches. The matter resolved via a federal consent order imposing a $100,000 civil penalty, a permanent ban on sharing health data for advertising, mandatory data deletion, and a 20-year privacy program with biennial third-party assessments.

## Background

Easy Healthcare developed the Premom Ovulation Tracker app, which collected menstrual cycle data, fertility status, pregnancy information, and hormone results from hundreds of thousands of users. Between 2017 and 2020, the company repeatedly promised users in its privacy policies that their health information would not be shared with third parties without consent and would be used only for Defendant's own analytics and advertising. In reality, it transmitted identifiable health data to Google and AppsFlyer via plain-text Custom App Events and shared sensitive data — including non-resettable device identifiers and precise geolocation — with Chinese analytics firms Umeng and Jiguang, which were contractually permitted to use the data for their own advertising. The company failed to assess SDK privacy risks, encrypt transferred data, or audit third-party practices, and never sent breach notifications despite experiencing breaches since at least 2017.

## Allegations

The FTC alleged eight counts: five privacy misrepresentation counts covering false promises about non-disclosure of health information, sharing only non-identifiable data, restricting third-party use to Defendant's own purposes, and deceiving users about geolocation collection; one count of unfair privacy and data security practices for incorporating third-party SDKs without risk assessment; one count of unfair sharing of health information for advertising without affirmative express consent; and one count of violating the Health Breach Notification Rule by failing to notify consumers, the FTC, and media outlets of breaches affecting PHR identifiable health information.

## Outcome

A federal consent order was entered requiring: a permanent prohibition on disclosing health information to third parties for advertising purposes; permanent ban on privacy misrepresentations; mandatory affirmative express consent before any non-advertising disclosure of health information; required breach notifications going forward; user notice posted for six months and emailed to covered users; deletion of covered information by specified third parties (Jiguang, Umeng, AppsFlyer, Google); a comprehensive privacy and information security program implemented within 60 days; biennial independent third-party privacy assessments for 20 years; annual sworn compliance certifications for 20 years; and a $100,000 civil penalty payable to the U.S. Treasury.

## Significance

This action marked the FTC's first use of the Health Breach Notification Rule as a basis for a law enforcement action, establishing that unauthorized disclosures of personal health record information to third-party SDKs and analytics firms constitute reportable breaches under the Rule. The case also set a precedent for scrutinizing reproductive health apps' data-sharing practices and their use of third-party SDKs, and demonstrated the FTC's willingness to pursue permanent advertising-data prohibitions as a remedy in health privacy cases.
