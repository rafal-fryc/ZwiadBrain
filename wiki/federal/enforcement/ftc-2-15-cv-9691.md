## Summary

The FTC charged LAI Systems, LLC (d/b/a TapBlaze), a Los Angeles mobile app developer, with violating COPPA by allowing third-party ad networks to collect persistent identifiers from children under 13 through its kids' apps for targeted advertising without parental notice or consent. The company failed to notify ad networks that its apps were directed to children, failed to post adequate privacy notices, and never obtained verifiable parental consent. The matter was resolved through a federal consent order imposing a $60,000 civil penalty and a permanent injunction.

## Background

LAI Systems operated children's apps — including My Cake Shop, My Pizza Shop, Hair Salon Makeover, Friday Night Makeover, Marley the Talking Dog, and Animal Sounds — that were clearly directed to children under age 13. Since at least July 1, 2013, LAI allowed third-party advertising networks to collect persistent identifiers from child users for targeted advertising without ever notifying those networks that the apps were child-directed.

## Allegations

The FTC alleged one count: LAI violated the COPPA Rule by operating children's-directed online services through which personal information in the form of persistent identifiers was collected from children under 13 by third-party advertising networks, without providing direct parental notice, posting a prominent privacy notice, or obtaining verifiable parental consent.

## Outcome

A federal consent order was entered against LAI Systems requiring payment of $60,000 in civil penalties within 7 days, a permanent injunction barring future COPPA Rule violations, mandatory order acknowledgments from personnel, annual compliance reporting for 10 years, five-year recordkeeping obligations, and FTC compliance monitoring authority.

## Significance

The action illustrates the FTC's enforcement posture on children's app developers who use third-party ad SDKs without segregating child-directed inventory — holding the app operator responsible for the data collection practices of ad networks integrated into their products, even when the operator did not itself directly collect the data.
