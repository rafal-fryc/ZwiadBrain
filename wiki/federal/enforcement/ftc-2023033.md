## Summary

The FTC charged Avast Limited, Avast Software s.r.o., and their subsidiary Jumpshot, Inc. with secretly collecting consumers' detailed browsing histories through antivirus and privacy-protection software and selling that data to over 100 third parties — while falsely claiming the products would block tracking and that any shared data would be anonymized and aggregated. The matter resolved via a consent order requiring Avast to pay $16.5 million, delete all Jumpshot data, and implement a 20-year privacy program subject to third-party audits.

## Background

From 2014 through January 2020, Avast's browser extensions and antivirus products collected granular clickstream data linked to persistent device identifiers. Respondents sold this data — revealing health, religious, political, and financial information — to advertising, data broker, and analytics companies in non-aggregate, re-identifiable form. Jumpshot retained over eight petabytes of this data indefinitely without deletion, and privacy policies falsely represented that transferred data would be anonymized and aggregated.

## Allegations

The FTC alleged three counts: (1) unfair collection, indefinite retention, and sale of consumers' browsing information in granular form; (2) deceptive failure to disclose tracking, given public representations that the software would stop such collection and sale; and (3) misrepresentations that browsing data transferred to Jumpshot and third parties would be in aggregate and anonymous form only.

## Outcome

Avast agreed to a consent order requiring payment of $16,500,000 to the FTC, deletion of all Jumpshot data and any derived models or algorithms within 20 days, notice to affected users, establishment of a comprehensive privacy program within 60 days, biennial independent third-party privacy assessments for 20 years, annual sworn compliance certifications published on Avast's website, and a permanent ban on selling or using browsing information for advertising purposes without affirmative express consent.

## Significance

The action is a significant FTC enforcement precedent establishing that marketing software as a privacy tool while covertly monetizing the very data it purports to protect constitutes both unfair data practices and deceptive trade practices under Section 5. The $16.5 million penalty and 20-year compliance regime signal the FTC's willingness to pursue antivirus and security vendors whose business models contradict their privacy representations.
