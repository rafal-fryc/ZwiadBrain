## Summary

The FTC charged TinyCo, Inc. with violating COPPA by collecting tens of thousands of email addresses from children under 13 through child-directed mobile gaming apps without notifying parents or obtaining verifiable parental consent. TinyCo operated apps such as Tiny Pets, Tiny Zoo, and Tiny Monsters, and sometimes incentivized children with in-app currency to submit their email addresses. The matter was resolved through a federal court consent order requiring TinyCo to pay a $300,000 civil penalty, delete all previously collected children's personal information, and comply with COPPA going forward.

## Background

TinyCo operated mobile gaming apps directed to children under 13, including Tiny Pets, Tiny Zoo, Tiny Village, Tiny Monsters, and Mermaid Resort. Through these apps, TinyCo collected tens of thousands of email addresses from children, sometimes incentivizing them with free in-app currency. Despite receiving complaints from parents and being aware that many users were under 13, TinyCo failed to provide required privacy notices, notify parents directly, or obtain verifiable parental consent before collecting personal information.

## Allegations

The FTC alleged a single count of COPPA Rule violations: TinyCo collected and used personal information online from children younger than 13 through its kids' apps without providing required notices to parents and without obtaining verifiable parental consent, in violation of Sections 1303(c) and 1306(d) of COPPA and Section 5 of the FTC Act.

## Outcome

The court entered a consent order permanently enjoining TinyCo from violating the COPPA Rule, requiring deletion of all personal information previously collected from children within ten days of order entry, and imposing a $300,000 civil penalty payable to the U.S. Treasury. TinyCo is also subject to ten-year recordkeeping obligations, compliance reporting, and ongoing monitoring by the FTC.

## Significance

This action illustrates that COPPA liability attaches even when children are incentivized—rather than simply invited—to submit personal information, and underscores that actual knowledge of a young user base (demonstrated here by parental complaints) eliminates any ambiguity defense.
