## Summary

The FTC charged D-Link Systems and its parent with failing to secure the software in consumer routers and IP cameras against well-known vulnerabilities — including hard-coded credentials, command injection flaws, and backdoors — while simultaneously marketing the devices as secure. D-Link also exposed its private code-signing key publicly for six months and stored mobile app login credentials in plaintext. The matter was resolved through a 20-year consent order requiring a comprehensive software security program and biennial third-party assessments.

## Background

D-Link Corporation and its U.S. subsidiary D-Link Systems, Inc. marketed routers and IP cameras to consumers for home network protection and access to sensitive personal information. Despite known software vulnerabilities, including hard-coded credentials, command injection flaws, and a backdoor, D-Link promoted the devices as secure and easy to secure. The company also inadvertently posted its private code-signing key on a public website for approximately six months and stored users' mobile app login credentials in clear readable text, leaving thousands of consumer devices exposed to remote attackers capable of redirecting web traffic, stealing financial information, hijacking cameras, or enlisting devices into botnets.

## Allegations

The FTC alleged six counts under FTC Act Section 5(a): (1) unfair security practices for failing to take reasonable steps to secure router and IP camera software; (2) misrepresentation through D-Link's Security Event Response Policy that the company took reasonable steps to secure its products; (3-4) promotional misrepresentations that routers and IP cameras were secure from unauthorized access; and (5-6) graphical user interface misrepresentations on both routers and IP cameras that the devices were secure from unauthorized access and control.

## Outcome

The consent order, entered July 15, 2019, requires D-Link Systems to establish, implement, and maintain a comprehensive software security program for 20 years covering its devices; obtain biennial independent third-party assessments of the program; submit annual sworn compliance certifications from senior management; stop selling the IP camera set-up wizard software containing specified misrepresentations and notify registered consumers to update firmware within 60 days; and maintain specified records for 10 years.

## Significance

This action extended FTC Section 5 data security enforcement to IoT device manufacturers, establishing that inadequate software security practices in consumer-facing hardware — combined with deceptive security claims — constitute an unfair or deceptive trade practice, and setting a precedent for mandatory independent security assessments in the IoT sector. Notably, D-Link litigated rather than settled immediately — the case was filed in January 2017 and resolved in July 2019.
