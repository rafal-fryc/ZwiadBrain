## Summary

The FTC alleged that Apitor Technology, a Chinese maker of programmable robot toys for children, secretly collected precise geolocation data from child users through a third-party SDK embedded in its Android app without notifying parents or obtaining verifiable parental consent. The company's Privacy Policy affirmatively misrepresented that no personal information was uploaded to external servers. A consent order was entered imposing a $500,000 civil penalty judgment (suspended on financial hardship grounds) and permanent injunctive relief.

## Background

Apitor Technology Co., Ltd. develops and sells programmable robot toys marketed to children ages 6 to 14, which require a companion mobile app (Apitor Kit) to operate. The Android version of the app integrated a third-party SDK called JPush that collected precise geolocation data from child users in the background and transmitted it to JPush servers without parental knowledge.

## Allegations

The FTC charged Apitor with a single count of failing to provide direct notice to parents of its data collection practices, failing to post a sufficient privacy notice on its website or online services, and failing to obtain verifiable parental consent before collecting personal information—including precise geolocation data—from children under age 13, in violation of the COPPA Rule. The company's Privacy Policy compounded the violation by affirmatively misrepresenting that no personal information was uploaded to external servers.

## Outcome

The court entered a consent order permanently restraining Apitor from future COPPA violations, including requirements to provide parental notice, obtain verifiable parental consent, honor parental deletion requests, and limit retention of children's data. Apitor was required to delete all previously collected personal information from affected child users within 60 days. A $500,000 civil penalty judgment was entered but suspended based on Defendant's sworn financial representations, subject to reinstatement if material misstatements are discovered. The order carries 10-year recordkeeping and annual compliance reporting obligations.

## Significance

The action highlights FTC enforcement against foreign-based children's app developers that embed third-party SDKs performing undisclosed data collection, and demonstrates that a Privacy Policy's affirmative misrepresentation of data practices—not merely silence—can support COPPA liability. The suspended penalty structure also illustrates the FTC's use of financial-hardship suspensions while preserving the ability to reinstate the full judgment.
