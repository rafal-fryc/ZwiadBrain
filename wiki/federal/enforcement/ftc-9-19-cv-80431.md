## Summary

The FTC alleged that Office Depot and its tech-support vendor Support.com used a fake PC Health Check Program that automatically generated false malware-detection results to pressure consumers into purchasing unnecessary repair services costing over $300 each. The program's alarming findings were predetermined by generic checkbox selections at intake, not by any actual scan. A consent order was finalized on February 15, 2020, requiring Office Depot to pay $25 million and permanently prohibiting misrepresentations about detected security issues.

## Background

From at least 2009 to November 2016, Office Depot and OfficeMax stores offered a free PC Health Check Program advertised as a diagnostic scan for viruses and performance issues. The software was designed so that checking any of four generic intake questions automatically triggered a false 'Malware Symptoms Found' result regardless of the computer's actual condition. Consumers were never told results were predetermined. Despite internal employee complaints and warnings from the Florida Attorney General, the practice continued until a Seattle TV station exposed it in late 2016.

## Allegations

Count 1 charged Office Depot with deceptive representations by falsely claiming the PC Health Check Program had detected malware infections or symptoms on consumers' computers. Count 2 charged Support.com with providing the means and instrumentalities of those false representations by supplying the deceptive program.

## Outcome

Office Depot entered a consent order imposing a $25 million monetary judgment payable to the FTC within 14 days of entry, permanent injunction against misrepresenting detection of security or performance issues on consumer devices, obligations to review and terminate non-compliant software providers, and 10-year recordkeeping and compliance-reporting requirements.

## Significance

The action established that a retailer's use of a pre-scripted diagnostic tool that fabricates security threat findings to drive service sales constitutes a deceptive practice under FTC Act Section 5, and that the software vendor supplying such a tool faces liability as a means-and-instrumentalities provider. The $25 million penalty underscores the FTC's willingness to pursue substantial monetary relief for fake cybersecurity-capability claims.
