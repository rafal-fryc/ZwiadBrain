## Summary

The FTC alleged that Sears Holdings Management Corporation secretly installed software on consumers' computers that collected far more data than disclosed — including sensitive financial and health information from secure sessions — while describing the program as simple 'online browsing' research. The matter originated as a 2009 consent order and was later reopened and modified in 2018 to narrow the definition of 'Tracking Application' in the original order.

## Developments

- 2018-02-15 — FTC reopened and modified the 2009 consent order against Sears Holdings Management Corporation to narrow the definition of 'Tracking Application,' resolving ambiguity in the original order's scope. — [source](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/082-3099-c-4264-sears-holdings-management-corporation-corporation-matter)

## Background

SHMC operated a 'My SHC Community' market research program from approximately April 2007 through January 2008, inviting consumers to download software described only as tracking 'online browsing.' Once installed, the application ran silently with no visible icon and transmitted nearly all internet activity — including online banking statements, drug prescription records, video rental histories, library borrowing histories, and web-based email headers — to SHMC's servers. The full scope of data collection was disclosed only approximately 75 lines deep in a scroll box within a lengthy Privacy Statement and User License Agreement.

## Allegations

The FTC charged one count of deceptive failure to disclose the scope of the tracking software under Section 5(a) of the FTC Act. SHMC represented, expressly or by implication, that the application would track only 'online browsing,' suggesting limited data collection, while the software in fact captured extensive sensitive data from secure sessions without prominent disclosure.

## Outcome

The Commission reopened the original 2009 consent order (Docket No. C-4264) and issued a modified order narrowing the definition of 'Tracking Application' to exclude software that monitors, records, or transmits only its own configuration, information about whether it is functioning as represented, or information about consumers' use of the program itself.

## Significance

This action illustrates the FTC's willingness to reopen and modify finalized consent orders to account for changed circumstances or definitional ambiguity. It also underscores that burying material disclosures about the scope of data collection deep within lengthy legal agreements does not satisfy the requirement for clear and conspicuous disclosure under Section 5.
