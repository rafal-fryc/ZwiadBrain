## Summary

The FTC charged Nomi Technologies with deceptively promising consumers an opt-out from its mobile device tracking service at retail locations while never providing any such in-store opt-out mechanism. The company's "Listen" service collected MAC addresses from shoppers' mobile devices to supply analytics to retailers without disclosing its presence to consumers. A 20-year consent order prohibits Nomi from misrepresenting consumer control over data collection and imposes recordkeeping and compliance reporting obligations.

## Background

Nomi Technologies operated a mobile device tracking service called "Listen" that collected MAC addresses and other data from consumers' mobile devices in retail locations to provide analytics to retailers. From November 2012 to October 2013, Nomi's privacy policy promised consumers they could opt out both on Nomi's website and at any retailer using Nomi's technology. However, Nomi never provided any opt-out mechanism at retail locations, nor did it ensure retailers disclosed to consumers that the Listen service was being used.

## Allegations

The FTC alleged two counts: (1) Nomi falsely represented that consumers could opt out of the Listen tracking service at retail locations, when no such mechanism existed in stores; and (2) Nomi falsely represented that consumers would receive notice when a retail location was using the Listen service, when no such notice was provided. Both counts charged deceptive acts or practices under Section 5(a) of the FTC Act.

## Outcome

A final consent order (Docket C-4538) was issued on September 15, 2015, effective for 20 years (terminating August 28, 2035). Nomi is permanently prohibited from misrepresenting the options through which consumers can exercise control over data collection or the extent to which consumers will receive notice about data practices. The order also imposes recordkeeping, compliance reporting, order distribution, and corporate-change notification requirements. No monetary penalty was assessed.

## Significance

This action established that companies deploying passive surveillance technology in physical retail spaces are bound by their own stated privacy promises, including opt-out mechanisms they advertise but never build. It signaled FTC scrutiny of location-analytics and device-tracking vendors — not just the retailers deploying them — and reinforced that deceptive privacy policy representations about consumer control are actionable under Section 5 even absent underlying statutory privacy requirements.
