## Summary

The FTC charged Henry Schein Practice Solutions with deceptively marketing its Dentrix G5 dental office software as providing industry-standard encryption for patient data when it actually used a weaker, proprietary algorithm. The company was also charged with falsely claiming the software helped dentists meet HIPAA compliance obligations. The matter was resolved through a consent order requiring a $250,000 payment, mandatory customer notification, and a 20-year prohibition on misrepresentations about encryption and security.

## Background

Henry Schein marketed Dentrix G5 dental office software as offering encryption for patient data and as helping dentists satisfy HIPAA requirements. As early as November 2010 the company knew the data protection relied on a proprietary, untested algorithm weaker than AES. In June 2013, US-CERT publicly identified the technology as a 'weak obfuscation algorithm,' yet Henry Schein continued marketing it as encryption for an additional seven months. The false claims were material because dentists relying on them might mistakenly believe they qualified for the HIPAA Breach Notification Rule's encryption safe harbor, potentially failing to notify patients after a data breach.

## Allegations

The FTC brought two counts of deceptive practices under FTC Act Section 5: (1) falsely representing that Dentrix G5 provides industry-standard encryption for patient data, and (2) falsely representing that the software helps dentists protect patient data as required by HIPAA.

## Outcome

Henry Schein was ordered to pay $250,000 to the FTC, provide clear and conspicuous notification to affected customers that Dentrix G5 uses a weaker algorithm than AES, maintain advertising records for five years, and submit to a 20-year prohibition on misrepresenting the encryption capabilities or security protections of any product that collects or stores personal information. The order terminates May 20, 2036.

## Significance

This case established that false encryption-strength claims for healthcare software constitute deceptive acts under FTC Act Section 5, even when the vendor knew of the weakness years before action was taken. It also illustrated that continuing to market a product as compliant after a public vulnerability disclosure (US-CERT) compounds the deception and can affect health data breach notification obligations under HIPAA.
