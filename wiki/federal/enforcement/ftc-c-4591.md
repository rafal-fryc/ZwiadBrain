## Summary

The FTC alleged that Practice Fusion deceived patients by collecting post-visit satisfaction survey responses under the impression they would be shared privately with their doctors, then publicly posting approximately 613,000 reviews — including sensitive health information — on a physician rating website without adequate prior disclosure. The consent order prohibits future misrepresentations about how consumer health information is used and requires affirmative express consent before making any covered information publicly available. The order also required Practice Fusion to remove existing publicly displayed healthcare provider reviews. The order runs for 20 years.

## Developments

- 2016-08-15 — Consent order issued requiring Practice Fusion to cease misrepresenting the private nature of patient satisfaction surveys and to obtain affirmative express consent before publicly posting any covered consumer health information. — [source](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/142-3039-practice-fusion-inc-matter)

## Background

Practice Fusion operated a cloud-based electronic health record service and beginning in April 2012 emailed post-visit satisfaction surveys to patients on behalf of their healthcare providers. Survey emails were framed as tools to help improve service and signed in the provider's name, leading patients to believe responses would go privately to their doctors. A 'Keep this review anonymous' checkbox falsely implied it would anonymize the consumer's identity when it only affected the publicly displayed name. In April 2013, Practice Fusion publicly posted all approximately 613,000 collected reviews on its Patient Fusion provider directory, exposing patients' names, phone numbers, medications, diagnoses, and treatment details.

## Allegations

The FTC charged one count of deceptive failure to disclose public posting of survey responses under FTC Act Section 5, alleging that from April 5, 2012 through April 8, 2013, Practice Fusion represented — directly, indirectly, expressly, or by implication — that survey responses would be communicated privately to the consumer's healthcare provider, when in fact they were later posted publicly.

## Outcome

Practice Fusion entered into a consent order (Docket C-4591, issued August 15, 2016) that prohibits misrepresentations about the privacy and confidentiality of covered information, requires clear disclosure and affirmative express consent before making consumer information publicly available, mandates removal of existing publicly displayed healthcare provider reviews, and imposes 20 years of compliance reporting, recordkeeping, and FTC monitoring obligations. No monetary penalty was imposed.

## Significance

This action established that misrepresenting the private nature of patient health surveys — and then publicly posting sensitive health information collected under those false pretenses — constitutes an unfair or deceptive act under FTC Act Section 5. It signaled FTC scrutiny of health-tech platforms' data-sharing practices even absent a formal data breach, and set a precedent requiring affirmative express consent before publicly disclosing sensitive health information collected through seemingly private channels.
