## Summary

The FTC charged Uber Technologies with making false claims about its data security practices, including misrepresenting that it closely monitored employee access to consumer data and used industry-standard security measures. In reality, Uber stored sensitive personal information unencrypted, used a single shared AWS key with full administrative privileges, and lacked multi-factor authentication. These failures contributed to a 2014 breach exposing over 100,000 driver records and a 2016 breach affecting approximately 25.6 million consumers, which Uber concealed from affected consumers and the FTC for over a year. The matter was resolved through a 20-year consent order requiring a comprehensive privacy program and biennial third-party assessments.

## Developments

- 2018-10-15 — FTC issued 20-year consent order against Uber for falsely claiming rigorous data access monitoring and industry-standard security while concealing a 2016 breach affecting 25.6 million consumers. — [source](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/152-3054-c-4662-uber-technologies-inc-matter)

## Background

Uber collected extensive personal information from riders and drivers, including Social Security numbers, bank account details, driver's license numbers, and precise geolocation data. Following a November 2014 public controversy over improper employee access to consumer data, Uber publicly claimed it strictly monitored and audited internal data access on an ongoing basis, but failed to implement effective monitoring systems for most of the following two years. Uber also stored sensitive personal information in plain unencrypted text in its Amazon S3 Datastore, used a single shared AWS access key with full administrative privileges, and lacked multi-factor authentication.

## Allegations

The FTC alleged two counts under FTC Act Section 5: (1) that Uber falsely claimed internal access to consumers' personal information is closely monitored and audited by data security specialists on an ongoing basis, when in fact it failed to implement effective monitoring systems; and (2) that Uber falsely claimed it would provide reasonable security for consumers' personal information through encryption, firewalls, SSL, and other commercially reasonable practices, when its actual practices fell materially short of those representations.

## Outcome

Uber entered into a 20-year consent order (Docket C-4662, effective October 15, 2018, terminating October 25, 2038) requiring it to: prohibit further misrepresentations about its data access monitoring and security practices; establish and maintain a comprehensive written privacy program; obtain biennial third-party privacy assessments by a qualified independent professional for 20 years; report covered security incidents to the FTC within 10 days of notifying any U.S. government entity; and maintain detailed records for 20 years subject to FTC compliance monitoring.

## Significance

This action underscores the FTC's position that material misrepresentations about data security practices — including concealing a major breach from regulators for over a year — constitute unfair or deceptive acts under Section 5. The 20-year order with mandatory third-party assessments reflects the FTC's use of extended oversight periods for companies with repeated or egregious security failures, and the incident-reporting provision directly addressed Uber's concealment of the 2016 breach.
