## Summary

The FTC alleged that Unrollme Inc. deceived users of its email management service by assuring them it would never access their personal emails while secretly granting its parent company, Slice Technologies, full inbox access to harvest and sell e-receipt data. The complaint charged two counts of deceptive acts under FTC Act Section 5. The matter resolved in a 20-year consent order requiring Unrollme to halt misrepresentations, notify affected consumers, and delete improperly collected data.

## Background

Unrollme provided email management services requiring full inbox access. It then gave parent company Slice Technologies access to those inboxes to collect e-receipt bodies — including names, addresses, credit card information, and purchase data — which Slice sold for market research. When consumers expressed privacy concerns, Unrollme representatives falsely claimed the service only accessed subscription emails.

## Allegations

Count 1 alleged that Unrollme falsely represented it would never 'touch' users' personal emails, displaying messages assuring consumers 'we won't touch your personal stuff' while secretly allowing full inbox harvesting. Count 2 alleged that Unrollme misrepresented its inbox access as limited to scanning for subscription emails, concealing the e-receipt data collection entirely from consumers and customer support inquiries.

## Outcome

A 20-year consent order (Docket C-4692) was issued on 2019-12-15. Unrollme was prohibited from misrepresenting how it accesses, collects, uses, stores, or shares consumer emails. It was required to notify all active consumers whose purchase receipts were accessed for market research within 10 days, and to delete all stored e-receipt data collected from consumers who enrolled before June 1, 2017, unless those consumers provided affirmative express consent. The order also imposes recordkeeping, compliance reporting, and monitoring obligations for 20 years.

## Significance

The case illustrates the FTC's willingness to act against deceptive privacy practices where a company's public-facing assurances are directly contradicted by its internal data-sharing arrangements. It highlights that consumer-facing reassurances — even informal phrases like 'we won't touch your personal stuff' — can constitute actionable deceptive representations under Section 5, and that data collected in breach of such promises may be subject to mandatory deletion orders.
