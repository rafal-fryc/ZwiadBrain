## Summary

The FTC charged InfoTrax Systems and its CEO Mark Rawlins with failing to implement reasonable data security measures for the sensitive personal and financial information of approximately 11.8 million consumers. An intruder exploited the deficiencies to access InfoTrax servers undetected seventeen times over nearly two years, ultimately exfiltrating data on roughly one million consumers and triggering over 280 fraud reports. The matter was resolved through a finalized FTC consent order requiring a comprehensive information security program, biennial third-party assessments, and twenty years of compliance obligations.

## Background

InfoTrax operated a backend technology platform for multi-level marketing companies, storing sensitive consumer data including Social Security numbers, payment card numbers, and bank account numbers. From at least 2014 through 2016, the company lacked basic controls such as input validation, network segmentation, intrusion detection, and encryption of stored credentials, enabling repeated undetected intrusions.

## Allegations

The FTC alleged a single count of unfairness under Section 5(a) of the FTC Act: Respondents assumed contractual responsibility for protecting consumers' personal information while operating a platform that stored sensitive data for approximately 11.8 million consumers without adequate security safeguards, resulting in a hacker accessing servers seventeen times and exfiltrating personal information for roughly one million consumers, over 2,300 full payment card numbers, and thousands of cleartext credentials.

## Outcome

A finalized consent order (Docket C-4696) required InfoTrax and its CEO to establish and maintain a comprehensive information security program, obtain initial and biennial independent third-party security assessments, submit annual compliance certifications and covered-incident reports to the FTC, and maintain detailed records for twenty years. The order is effective for twenty years from issuance.

## Significance

The action illustrates the FTC's use of Section 5 unfairness authority to hold backend B2B technology processors — not just consumer-facing companies — liable for data security failures, and imposed individual liability on the CEO alongside the corporate entity. The seventeen-intrusion, two-year detection failure underscored the agency's focus on basic, well-established security controls such as intrusion detection and network segmentation.
