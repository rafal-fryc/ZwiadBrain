## Summary

The FTC charged Retina-X Studios, LLC and its sole member James N. Johns, Jr. with selling covert mobile device monitoring apps that enabled stalking while falsely claiming user data was kept private and secure. Two hacker breaches in 2017 and 2018 exposed sensitive data, and the company's TeenShield product collected personal information from children under 13 without adequate safeguards in violation of COPPA. The matter was resolved through a 20-year consent order imposing sweeping restrictions and a mandatory information security program.

## Background

Retina-X developed and sold MobileSpy, PhoneSheriff, and TeenShield — apps that could run surreptitiously on victims' devices to secretly capture GPS location, text messages, photos, and browser history. Despite marketing assurances of confidentiality and security, the company suffered two data breaches in 2017 and 2018 that exposed and erased sensitive user data. The TeenShield product was used with actual knowledge that children under 13 were being monitored, yet no reasonable procedures were in place to protect their personal information.

## Allegations

The FTC alleged five counts: (1) unfair sale of surreptitious monitoring products that circumvented device security without steps to ensure lawful use; (2–4) deceptive data security representations for each of the three products, falsely promising that user data was confidential, private, and safe; and (5) COPPA violations through TeenShield's collection of personal information from children under 13 without reasonable protective procedures.

## Outcome

Respondents agreed to a 20-year consent order permanently barring them from selling monitoring products without compliance safeguards, notice requirements, and attestations of lawful use; prohibiting misrepresentations about data security; requiring deletion of previously collected personal information within 120 days; mandating a comprehensive written information security program; and requiring biennial third-party security assessments, annual certifications, covered-incident reporting within 10 days, and 10-year recordkeeping obligations. The order terminates March 26, 2040.

## Significance

The case is a landmark FTC action against stalkerware, establishing that selling covert monitoring tools without reasonable safeguards against misuse is an unfair practice under the FTC Act, and combining data-security, COPPA, and anti-stalking theories in a single consent order.
