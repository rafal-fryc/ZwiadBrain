## Summary

The FTC charged SkyMed International, Inc., a seller of emergency travel membership plans, with displaying a self-created 'HIPAA Compliance' seal that falsely implied government or third-party verification of its privacy practices. The company also left approximately 130,000 consumer records — including sensitive health information — exposed in an unsecured cloud database for over five months, then falsely told affected consumers that no health data had been exposed. The matter was resolved through a 20-year consent order requiring a comprehensive information security program, biennial third-party assessments, and prohibitions on misrepresenting data security practices.

## Background

SkyMed International sold emergency travel membership plans and collected extensive personal information from consumers, including sensitive health data such as medical conditions, prescriptions, and hospitalization history. From 2014 to April 2019 the company displayed a self-created 'HIPAA Compliance' seal on every page of its website. No government agency or third party had ever reviewed its practices. An unsecured cloud database containing roughly 130,000 consumer records was left publicly accessible for over five months; when notifying consumers, SkyMed falsely claimed its investigation found no health information was exposed, when in fact it had simply deleted the database without examining its contents.

## Allegations

The FTC alleged three counts: (1) misrepresentation through the 'HIPAA Compliance' seal falsely implying verified compliance; (2) misrepresentation in the breach notification falsely claiming health information was not stored on or accessed from the exposed database; and (3) unfair information security practices for failing to develop written security policies, train employees, store data securely, conduct risk assessments, inventory and delete unnecessary data, or use data-loss prevention tools.

## Outcome

SkyMed entered a 20-year consent order (Docket C-4732) prohibiting misrepresentations about privacy or security programs and breach investigations. The order requires establishment of a comprehensive information security program within 30 days, biennial third-party security assessments, annual sworn compliance certifications, covered-incident reporting to the FTC within 30 days of discovery, consumer breach notification, and 20-year recordkeeping. No monetary penalty is stated in the text.

## Significance

The case illustrates the FTC's use of Section 5 to address both deceptive security-seal claims and affirmative misrepresentations made during breach response — holding companies accountable not only for the underlying security failure but also for misleading consumers about what was actually exposed. It reinforces that deleting breach evidence rather than investigating it can itself constitute a deceptive act.
