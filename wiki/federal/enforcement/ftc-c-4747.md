## Summary

The FTC alleged that Flo Health, operator of a popular period and ovulation tracking app, secretly shared users' intimate reproductive health data with Facebook, Google, and other third parties despite repeatedly promising it would not do so. The company also falsely claimed to comply with EU-U.S. and Swiss-U.S. Privacy Shield principles. The matter was resolved through a 20-year consent order requiring data deletion, user notification, affirmative consent before future health-data disclosures, and independent privacy compliance review.

## Background

Between 2016 and 2019, Flo Health embedded third-party SDKs from Facebook, Google, Fabric, AppsFlyer, and Flurry in its app and transmitted users' health information to those parties without contractual limits on downstream use. The practice was exposed by a Wall Street Journal investigation in February 2019, after which Flo Health halted the sharing. The company had also certified participation in the EU-U.S. and Swiss-U.S. Privacy Shield frameworks while violating multiple Privacy Shield Principles.

## Allegations

The FTC charged seven counts of deceptive practices under FTC Act Section 5: three counts of privacy misrepresentation (falsely promising not to share health data with third parties, falsely claiming only non-personally identifiable information was shared with certain partners, and falsely representing that third-party use was restricted to in-app services), and four counts of Privacy Shield misrepresentation covering the Notice, Choice, Accountability for Onward Transfers, and Data Integrity and Purpose Limitation principles.

## Outcome

The FTC issued a 20-year consent order requiring Flo Health to: instruct all third parties that received health information to destroy it within 30 days; provide conspicuous notice to users within 14 days; obtain affirmative express consent before any future health-data disclosures; commission an independent third-party compliance review of its Privacy Shield practices within 180 days; and maintain 20 years of records with annual compliance reporting for 5 years.

## Significance

This order marked one of the FTC's most prominent actions involving sensitive reproductive health data and established that secretly routing intimate health information through advertising SDKs — even without direct monetary exchange — constitutes a deceptive practice under Section 5. The data-deletion obligation directed at third parties was a notable structural remedy, and the case predated the post-Dobbs heightened scrutiny of reproductive data by several years.
