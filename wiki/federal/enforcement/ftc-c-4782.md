## Summary

The FTC charged Chegg, Inc. with failing to implement reasonable data security measures for years, resulting in at least four separate breaches that exposed personal information of approximately 40 million student users and W-2 data of roughly 700 employees. Chegg also falsely represented in its privacy policy that it took commercially reasonable steps to protect user data. The matter was resolved through a 20-year consent order requiring Chegg to implement a comprehensive information security program, offer multi-factor authentication, and submit to biennial third-party security assessments.

## Background

Chegg is an educational technology company serving high school and college students. From at least 2017, it neglected basic security controls including access management, encryption, multi-factor authentication, employee training, and data monitoring. These failures led to at least four security incidents, including a 2018 breach exposing data of approximately 40 million users and a 2020 breach exposing employee W-2 information, despite Chegg's privacy policy claiming commercially reasonable security protections.

## Allegations

The FTC alleged two counts: (1) unfair data security practices — that Chegg failed to employ reasonable and appropriate measures to protect personal information collected from users and employees; and (2) data security misrepresentations — that Chegg falsely represented, expressly or by implication, that it implemented reasonable measures to protect personal information against unauthorized access, in violation of Section 5(a) of the FTC Act.

## Outcome

Chegg entered a 20-year consent order (Docket C-4782) requiring it to: stop misrepresenting its data security practices; implement a comprehensive information security program within 90 days covering risk assessments, access controls, MFA, encryption, and vendor oversight; offer MFA to consumer users within six months; notify affected individuals of identified breaches; establish data retention and deletion procedures; obtain biennial independent third-party security assessments; and submit annual senior-officer compliance certifications to the FTC.

## Significance

The action underscores the FTC's continued use of Section 5 to hold ed-tech companies accountable for systemic, multi-year security failures affecting tens of millions of students. The mandatory MFA offering for consumers and the 20-year order duration signal heightened expectations for companies collecting sensitive personal data from younger, student populations.
