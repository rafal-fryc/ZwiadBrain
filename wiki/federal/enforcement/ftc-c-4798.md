## Summary

The FTC charged 1Health.io Inc. (also known as Vitagene), a DNA health test kit company, with falsely claiming industry-leading security while publicly exposing the genetic and health records of more than 2,600 consumers through unsecured cloud storage. The company also misrepresented its data deletion and saliva sample destruction practices, and in 2020 retroactively expanded third-party sharing of sensitive consumer data without consent. The matter was resolved through a consent order requiring a $75,000 civil penalty, a 20-year information security program, and prohibition on further misrepresentations.

## Background

1Health.io Inc., operating as Vitagene, sold DNA health test kits and related health reports. Between 2017 and 2019, the company ignored at least three warnings about unsecured Amazon S3 storage buckets that exposed the health and genetic data of over 2,600 consumers. Despite promising consumers that DNA results were stored without identifying information and that data could be deleted on request, the company lacked the systems and contracts to fulfill those promises. In April and December 2020, Vitagene retroactively revised its privacy policy to permit far broader third-party sharing of sensitive consumer data — including with pharmacies, supermarket chains, and nutrition retailers — without notifying or obtaining consent from existing customers.

## Allegations

The FTC alleged five counts: (1) misrepresenting that the company exceeded industry-standard security practices; (2) falsely claiming DNA results were stored without identifying information; (3) misrepresenting that consumers could delete all their data upon request; (4) falsely claiming physical DNA saliva samples were destroyed after analysis; and (5) unfairly applying retroactive privacy policy changes that materially expanded third-party sharing of sensitive health and genetic information without obtaining affirmative consent from existing customers.

## Outcome

The consent order required 1Health.io to pay $75,000, establish and maintain a comprehensive information security program for 20 years with biennial third-party assessments, obtain affirmative express consent before disclosing health information to third parties, instruct laboratories to destroy retained saliva samples within 30 days, and submit annual compliance certifications and covered incident reports to the FTC. The order is final and runs for 20 years from its issuance date.

## Significance

This case illustrates the FTC's use of both deception and unfairness theories against a direct-to-consumer genetic testing company. The retroactive privacy policy count — charging that applying expanded data-sharing terms to existing customers without consent was an unfair practice — represents a notable extension of FTC enforcement to post-collection data use changes for sensitive genetic and health information.
