## Summary

The FTC charged Marriott International and its subsidiary Starwood Hotels & Resorts with deceptive and unfair data security practices following three major breaches between 2014 and 2020 that exposed hundreds of millions of consumer records, including payment card data and unencrypted passport numbers. Despite repeatedly publishing privacy policies claiming reasonable security safeguards were in place, the companies failed to implement adequate protections. The matter was resolved through a 20-year consent order requiring a comprehensive information security program, biennial third-party assessments, annual CEO certifications, and consumer remedies including a loyalty account review process and data deletion rights.

## Background

Marriott International and its subsidiary Starwood Hotels & Resorts experienced three major data breaches spanning 2014 to 2020. The first breach (2014–2015) exposed payment card data for over 40,000 consumers due to inadequate firewalls, outdated software, and lack of multifactor authentication. The second breach went undetected for over four years (July 2014–September 2018), compromising 339 million global consumer records including 5.25 million unencrypted passport numbers. The third breach (September 2018–February 2020) exposed more than 5.2 million guest records. Throughout this period, both companies' privacy policies claimed they used appropriate and reasonable security safeguards.

## Allegations

The FTC alleged two counts: (1) deceptive security statements — that Marriott and Starwood misrepresented through their website privacy policies that they used appropriate safeguards to protect consumers' personal information; and (2) unfair information security practices — that Respondents failed to employ reasonable security measures to protect consumers' personal information, in violation of Section 5(a) of the FTC Act.

## Outcome

The matter was resolved through a 20-year consent order (Docket C-4807, issued October 15, 2024) requiring Respondents to: establish a comprehensive information security program within 180 days; obtain biennial independent third-party security assessments for 20 years; provide annual CEO compliance certifications; report covered incidents to the FTC within 10 days of notifying any U.S. government entity; offer U.S. consumers a method to review loyalty rewards accounts for unauthorized activity and restore lost points; implement a consumer data deletion request process; and maintain extensive recordkeeping for 20 years.

## Significance

This action is notable for its breadth — covering three separate multi-year breaches affecting hundreds of millions of consumers — and for the stringency of its remedies, including a 20-year oversight period, mandatory CEO-level annual certifications, and affirmative consumer rights to loyalty account review and data deletion. It reinforces the FTC's position that publishing aspirational privacy policy language without backing it with actual security practices constitutes deception under Section 5.
