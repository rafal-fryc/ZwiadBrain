## Summary

The FTC charged LabMD, a cancer-detection laboratory, with failing to implement reasonable data security practices, which allowed a file containing sensitive personally identifiable information of approximately 9,000 patients to become accessible on the LimeWire peer-to-peer network in 2008. The Commission initiated an administrative proceeding under Section 5 of the FTC Act in 2013. An ALJ dismissed the complaint in November 2015 for lack of consumer harm evidence, but the Commission reversed that dismissal in July 2016 and issued a Final Decision and Order.

## Developments

- 2016-09-15 — Commission issued its Final Decision and Order finding LabMD's failure to implement reasonable data security practices an unfair act or practice under FTC Act Section 5, reversing the ALJ's earlier dismissal. — [source](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/102-3099-labmd-inc-matter)

## Background

LabMD, Inc. operated as a cancer-detection laboratory. In 2008, a 1,718-page file containing sensitive PII of approximately 9,000 patients became accessible via the LimeWire peer-to-peer network. The file was obtained by Tiversa, Inc., a commercial data-security firm with a financial interest in exposing such breaches. The FTC initiated Docket No. 9357 in 2013. The Chief ALJ dismissed the complaint in November 2015 for insufficient evidence of substantial consumer harm; the Commission reversed in July 2016. Key evidence relied on by the FTC was later found by the ALJ to have been fabricated by Tiversa.

## Allegations

The FTC alleged that LabMD's failure to employ reasonable and appropriate measures to protect consumers' personal information on its computer networks — including permitting a sensitive patient data file to be exposed on the LimeWire peer-to-peer network — constituted an unfair act or practice in violation of Section 5 of the FTC Act.

## Outcome

The Commission reversed the ALJ's dismissal in July 2016 and issued a Final Decision and Order. (Note: the Eleventh Circuit subsequently vacated the Commission's order in 2018 as unenforceably vague — LabMD v. FTC, 894 F.3d 1221 — a landmark limit on FTC data-security remedial authority; this appellate outcome is outside the extracted order text.)

## Significance

The case established the FTC's authority to pursue data-security enforcement against businesses under the unfairness prong of Section 5, even in the absence of a consent order, through a fully litigated administrative adjudication. It also raised significant questions about the reliability of evidence supplied by third-party commercial security firms, and its later vacatur on appeal became the leading appellate precedent limiting the specificity of FTC data-security orders.
