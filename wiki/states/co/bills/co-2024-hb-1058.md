## Summary

HB 24-1058 amends the Colorado Privacy Act to classify biological data, explicitly including neural data, as sensitive personal data. Covered controllers must obtain opt-in consent before collecting or processing such data, making Colorado the first state to extend statutory privacy protections to neural and biological information. The amendment integrates neural data protection into Colorado's existing consumer privacy enforcement framework.

## Legislative History

### Legislative Journey

HB24-1058, "Protect Privacy of Biological Data," was introduced in the Colorado House on January 10, 2024, by prime sponsors Rep. Cathy Kipp and Rep. Matt Soper, with Senate prime sponsors Sen. Mark Baisley and Sen. Kevin Priola, and more than 30 additional cosponsors from both parties. The bill amends the Colorado Privacy Act to bring "biological data" -- including neural data generated by measuring nervous system activity -- within the statute's definition of "sensitive data," subjecting it to opt-in consent and heightened protection requirements. The House Judiciary Committee took up the bill and reported it out with amendment L.001 on January 30, 2024, by an 11-0 vote, and the House gave it Second Reading approval on February 2 before passing it on Third Reading, 61-1, on February 9, 2024, with floor amendment L.002 attached. The bill crossed to the Senate on February 12 and was assigned to the Business, Labor, & Technology Committee, which referred it favorably on a 6-0 vote on March 21, 2024. The Senate passed the bill on Second Reading on March 25 and on Third Reading by a unanimous 34-0 vote on March 26, 2024. Following enrollment -- signed by the House Speaker on April 11 and the Senate President on April 12 -- the bill was transmitted to the executive branch, and Governor Jared Polis signed it into law on April 17, 2024, enacting it as Chapter 68 of the 2024 session laws, with an effective date of August 7, 2024. The legislation moved through the General Assembly with broad, largely bipartisan support and only a single dissenting vote across both chambers, reflecting consensus on extending the state's landmark consumer privacy law to cover emerging biometric and neurotechnology data.
